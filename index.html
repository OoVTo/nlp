PART 1

Introduction to Natural Language Processing

Unstructured and structured text

Structured Text

Data organized in a predefined format, schema, or model — SQL databases, CSV files, spreadsheets — making it easily searchable and manageable by algorithms.

Unstructured Text

Data without a predefined conceptual model or structure — emails, social media posts, articles, videos — requiring processing techniques like NLP to analyze.

Core concepts of NLP

Natural Language UnderstandingNLU

Focuses on machine reading comprehension and interpreting human language intent, context, and semantics.

Natural Language GenerationNLG

Focuses on producing text or speech responses from structured data or internal representations.

Natural Language ProcessingNLP

The broader field combining NLU and NLG (NLP = NLU + NLG) to enable communication between machines and humans.

Applications of NLP

Virtual Assistants & Chatbots Machine Translation Sentiment Analysis Spam Detection

Key NLP operations and techniques

Tokenization

The process of breaking down a text into smaller units called tokens (e.g., words, subwords, or characters).

Tokens

Individual units or pieces of language — such as words, punctuation, or symbols — obtained through tokenization.

Stemming

The process of reducing words to their root or stem form by chopping off affixes (e.g., "running" → "run"), often using crude heuristic rules.

Lemmatization

The process of determining the dictionary base form, or lemma, of a word based on its intended context and morphological analysis (e.g., "better" → "good").

POS TaggingPart of Speech

The process of marking up a word in a text to correspond to a particular part of speech (noun, verb, adjective, etc.) based on both its definition and context.

Named Entity RecognitionNER

An information extraction task that identifies and classifies key entities in text into predefined categories such as person names, organizations, locations, dates, and quantities.

Text Normalization

The process of converting text into a standard, consistent format — lowercasing, expanding contractions, removing punctuation.

Stop Word Removal

The technique of filtering out common, high-frequency words (e.g., "the," "is," "at") that carry little domain-specific semantic value.

LESSON 1

Fundamentals of Natural Language

Natural language

Language spoken by humans that evolved naturally through use, repetition, and social communication. It is inherently distinct from artificial programming languages and formal logic syntax.

Characteristics

  1. Structured sounds and words — words are fixed components used for communication in speech, writing, and gesturing.
  2. Communication — represents a core capacity and expression of human intelligence.

Global linguistic challenges

  • Language Diversity
  • Language Barriers
Lingua Franca

A bridge language systematically used to make communication possible between groups of people who do not share a native language (e.g., English globally).

Natural Language Processing (NLP)

An interdisciplinary subfield of artificial intelligence, computer science, and linguistics focused on giving computers the ability to understand, interpret, process, and generate human language.

NLP = NLU + NLG
Natural Language UnderstandingNLU

Analyzes input language to enable machine comprehension, parsing intent, and structuring data for computational use.

Natural Language GenerationNLG

Translates computer data and knowledge into fluent, human-readable output text or speech.

Human cognitive analog of processing (NLU + NLG)

1
Conceptualization
Identifying concepts and ideas.
2
Formulation
Combining and structuring linguistic structures around concepts.
3
Articulation
Expressing the formulated linguistic structure via speech, writing, or sign.

Major NLP system challenges

  • Understanding context, like sarcasm
  • Limited reasoning
  • Bias and hallucination

History and Evolution of NLP

1950s–1960s

Early beginnings of NLP. Warren Weaver's "Translation Memorandum" treated translating languages like decoding a secret message. The idea was founded on Information Theory, which states that phrases are assumed to be related to one another.

1950s

Machine translation — the first application of NLP; it was simplistic.

1950

Alan Turing developed the Turing Test, administered to determine whether a machine could copy a human response, sentence, or way of speaking. It was later deemed flawed, because a machine could easily mimic the structure or way a human talks without truly understanding it.

1954

Georgetown–IBM experiment. Machine translation was applied to 49 Russian sentences translated into English, all related to chemistry — cherry-picked and scripted.

1957

Noam Chomsky brought forth "generative grammar": language doesn't have a fixed number of sentences — it's infinite. He also stated that words have logic: subject + word + object.

1966

ELIZA, a chatbot developed by Joseph Weizenbaum, was designed to act like a psychotherapist but was ineffective — it only echoed back using keywords, scripted rather than understood.

That same year, the U.S. government formed the ALPAC (Automatic Language Processing Advisory Committee) to evaluate the progress of NLP and computational linguistics. ALPAC judged NLP to be more expensive, slower, and less accurate than a human translator — triggering the AI Winter, in which NLP projects lost funding.

1970s

New concepts were introduced: Case Grammar, Semantic Network, and Conceptual Dependency Theory.

1980s

Expert Systems were introduced (symbolic approaches). Knowledgebases stored words, phrases, or sentences to predict the next words, phrases, or sentences.

Late 1980s–Early 1990s

Statistical approaches developed — the closest step yet to machine learning, identifying patterns in data.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs): a class of artificial neural networks that accept sequential data inputs and use internal memory loops to process sequences of variable length — critical for early neural translation and language modeling.

2000s

Word embeddings emerged, in which common or similar words are grouped together to determine their relationships.

2006

Google Translate launched.

Challenges of the era

  • Models were unable to understand relationships.
  • Models had only a few examples to learn from.
LESSON 3

Linguistic Complexity and Ambiguity

Why is natural language hard to process?

1. Ambiguity at multiple levels

  • Word Senses (Lexical): e.g., "bank" (financial institution vs. river bank)
  • Part of Speech: e.g., "chair" (noun: furniture vs. verb: to preside over a meeting)
  • Syntactic Structure: e.g., "I saw a man with a telescope" (who has the telescope?)
  • Quantifier Scope: e.g., "Every child loves some movie" (the same movie, or different movies?)
  • Multiple Ambiguities: e.g., "I saw her duck" (a crouching movement, or a bird she owns?)

NLP algorithms model this ambiguity, and choose the correct analysis in context.

2. Linguistic diversity and resource scarcity

  • 6,000–7,000 languages worldwide, categorized across more than 14 major language families.
  • Languages diverge across all levels of structural hierarchy — no universal plug-and-play solution exists for a single task across all languages.
  • Most languages lack sufficient digital corpora or annotated training resources required to build high-accuracy statistical or neural NLP models.
Sapir–Whorf Hypothesis

The linguistic principle proposing that the structure of a language influences or determines its speakers' worldview and cognitive processes.

Low-Resource Languages

Languages that lack large monolingual or parallel text corpora, dictionary benchmarks, or expert annotations necessary to build statistical and machine learning models.

Contextual & coreference resolution

The same pronoun can resolve to a different antecedent depending on a single word elsewhere in the sentence:

"The man couldn't lift his son because he was so weak."
→ "he" refers to the man
"The man couldn't lift his son because he was so heavy."
→ "he" refers to the son
"Jane made sure to thank Susan for all the help she had received."
→ "she" = Jane
"Jane made sure to thank Susan for all the help she had given."
→ "she" = Susan
"John promised Bill to leave so an hour later he left." (promised)
→ "he" = John
"John ordered Bill to leave so an hour later he left." (ordered)
→ "he" = Bill

Similarly, "Mary and Sue are sisters" versus "Mary and Sue are mothers" require evaluating different relationship structures between the same two entities.

Detailed classification of ambiguity types

Ambiguity

A fundamental challenge in NLP where words, phrases, or full sentences yield multiple valid computational interpretations. Example: "He found his train in the pen" — train could mean vehicle or toy; pen could mean writing tool or enclosure.

Occurs when an individual word possesses multiple distinct meanings.

Homonyms

Words that sound alike or are spelled alike but have different meanings.

bark — the sound a dog makes vs. the outer covering of a tree

Polysemy

A single word with multiple related meanings.

head — the top part of the body vs. leader of an organization

Occurs when a sentence can be parsed in multiple grammatical ways due to structural syntax.

Attachment

Ambiguity arises when it is unclear which part of the sentence a modifier (e.g., a prepositional phrase) is associated with.

He saw the man with the telescope.

Did he use a telescope to see the man, or did he see a man who had a telescope?

Coordination

Occurs when it is unclear how conjunctions like "and" or "or" connect different parts of a sentence.

Old men and women were admitted free.

Were only old men admitted free, or both old men and all women?

Occurs when a sentence has multiple interpretations even after structural and lexical resolution.

Quantifier Scope Ambiguity

Arises when it's unclear how far a quantifier (like "all" or "some") applies within a sentence.

All students read some books.

Does it mean each student reads the same books, or different books?

Anaphoric Ambiguity

Occurs when a pronoun or referring expression can be linked to multiple possible antecedents.

John told Peter he would win.

Is "he" referring to John or Peter?

Occurs when context, tone, or situational cues dictate meaning beyond the literal text.

Deictic Ambiguity

Arises when it is unclear who or what a deictic expression (like "this," "that," "here," "there") refers to.

I'll meet you there.

Where is "there"?

Irony / Sarcasm

Arises when the intended meaning is the opposite of the literal meaning.

Oh, great! Another meeting!

Linguistic variations in low-resource contexts

A lack of large annotated datasets and linguistic tools hinders the development of accurate NLP models for many languages, especially given how language varies below the level of "a language":

Dialectal variations

Distinct forms of a language spoken by specific groups, often distinguished by geographical regions. Each dialect can have unique vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation.

"truck" (American English) vs. "lorry" (British / Indian English) — same vehicle. Pronunciation of "tomato" (toh-MAH-toh vs. to-MAY-to) also differs between regions.

Sociolects

Variations in language used by specific social groups, often influenced by class, education, or occupation. The way language is used can signal social identity and status.

"Good morning, how are you?" (formal) vs. "Hey, what's up?" (informal). Professional jargon — legal, medical — is another sociolect.

Idiolects

The unique form of language used by an individual — personal preferences in word choice, pronunciation, and sentence structure, reflecting a person's background, experiences, and personality.

"you know," "like" — filler words one person uses frequently while another avoids them entirely. Some individuals also have distinctively their own phrases or expressions.
